**When spaced-repetition reviews pile up, pause most new cards, protect a small daily time block and work through the queue by priority—not guilt.** Do not reset the deck or attempt an exhausting all-day clear. A backlog is a workload signal, not a moral failure.

The goal is to restore a routine you can sustain.

## The 20-minute recovery plan

If you need a plan for today:

1. pause new cards;
2. set a 20-minute timer;
3. review the most important due cards;
4. answer honestly before revealing;
5. suspend irrelevant or broken cards;
6. stop when the timer ends;
7. repeat tomorrow.

If you have an urgent exam, prioritize its topics—but keep a time limit so review does not replace practice questions.

## Why review backlogs happen

### Too many new cards

Every new card creates future reviews. A sustainable week can become an impossible month when additions continue at the same rate.

### The cards take too long

Broad, ambiguous or essay-length prompts slow each session and produce repeated failure. The problem may be deck design, not discipline.

### Your schedule changed

Illness, travel, assignments, work and family responsibilities interrupt routines. A scheduler cannot know which obligation should win.

### The deck contains low-value material

When every detail enters the queue, important concepts compete with trivia. Avoidance is a predictable response to work that no longer feels useful.

### You used “again” without repairing the card

A card that repeatedly fails may lack context or test too much. Rescheduling the same weak prompt creates more work without resolving the cause.

## First, measure the backlog

Write down:

- number of overdue cards;
- normal reviews completed per minute;
- daily time available;
- exam or deadline dates;
- high-priority decks;
- number of new cards currently added each day.

Estimate:

> recovery sessions ≈ overdue cards ÷ cards completed per session

If 600 cards are overdue and you can carefully review 60 in 20 minutes, the naïve estimate is 10 sessions. Some cards will return sooner after failure, so allow extra time.

The calculation is not a promise. It turns a frightening number into a plan.

## Triage before you review

Not every overdue card deserves equal attention.

### Priority A: needed soon and difficult to replace

Examples:

- material for an upcoming exam;
- safety-critical knowledge;
- prerequisites for current coursework;
- language you use regularly.

Review these first.

### Priority B: useful, but not urgent

Maintain these after the A queue is stable.

### Priority C: obsolete, duplicated or optional

Suspend or delete these. A card should earn its place in your future schedule.

Suspending is useful when you are uncertain: it removes the item from daily review without destroying it.

## Pause new cards without pausing learning

Set the new-card limit to zero or a very small number while the queue is growing.

You can still:

- attend lectures;
- understand new material;
- solve problems;
- annotate likely future prompts;
- save candidate cards outside the active deck.

Resume additions only when daily reviews fit the available time. The guide to [daily flashcard limits](/blog/how-many-flashcards-per-day) includes a one-week test for setting a sustainable rate.

## Choose a recovery strategy

### Strategy 1: fixed daily time

Best for most students.

- review for 15–30 minutes;
- prioritize the relevant queue;
- stop on time;
- repeat every day;
- add no new cards until the trend improves.

This protects consistency and prevents flashcards from consuming the entire study plan.

### Strategy 2: fixed daily card count

Useful when card difficulty is fairly consistent.

- choose a count you can complete carefully;
- review that many overdue cards plus unavoidable current work;
- reduce the count if answer quality falls.

Do not use the number as a speed contest.

### Strategy 3: deadline triage

For an upcoming exam:

1. isolate the tested topics;
2. suspend unrelated decks temporarily;
3. review high-yield cards;
4. alternate with exam-format practice;
5. create only targeted cards from repeated errors.

You may not clear the global backlog before the exam. That is acceptable if the important learning is covered.

## Repair cards during recovery

Backlog sessions reveal deck problems. When a card is repeatedly slow or wrong, ask:

- Did I understand this before memorizing it?
- Does the prompt identify one target?
- Is the answer short enough to grade?
- Is necessary context missing?
- Is the card still relevant?
- Would a diagram, problem or explanation work better?

Then choose:

- **rewrite** an unclear prompt;
- **split** an overloaded card;
- **add context** or a source;
- **suspend** a low-priority card;
- **delete** a duplicate;
- **study** the concept before retrying it.

The [good flashcards checklist](/blog/how-to-make-good-flashcards) helps diagnose repeat offenders.

## What not to do

### Do not reset the whole deck immediately

Resetting can erase useful scheduling information and turn known material back into new material. First reduce additions, triage and work in bounded sessions. Export or back up before making major scheduler changes.

### Do not clear cards by revealing answers

Marking familiar-looking answers as known hides the backlog instead of rebuilding retrieval.

### Do not spend one punishing day on it

A marathon may produce fatigue, shallow answers and cards due together again. Recovery should create a daily rhythm.

### Do not keep every old card

Courses end, priorities change and some details were never useful. Curation is part of maintaining a learning system.

### Do not ignore sleep or urgent work

The queue is a tool-generated suggestion. It is not more important than health, deadlines or realistic exam practice.

## Prevent the next backlog

### Add cards gradually

Increase the new-card limit in small steps only after a stable week.

### Use focused batches

When generating from a [PDF](/pdf-to-flashcards) or lecture, start with one section. Inspect and select before activating the whole batch.

### Keep a maximum review time

When due cards repeatedly exceed the cap, reduce additions. Do not wait for the queue to become intimidating.

### Maintain the deck weekly

Spend five minutes:

- deleting duplicates;
- suspending obsolete cards;
- rewriting confusing prompts;
- checking whether the deck still serves a goal.

### Expect interruptions

Before travel or exams, reduce new cards in advance. After an interruption, restart with a small block rather than waiting for the perfect empty day.

## A seven-day example reset

| Day | New cards | Recovery action |
|---|---:|---|
| 1 | 0 | Measure, triage and review 20 minutes |
| 2 | 0 | Priority A for 20 minutes |
| 3 | 0 | Priority A; repair repeat failures |
| 4 | 0 | A then B; suspend obsolete cards |
| 5 | 0 | Review within the same time cap |
| 6 | 0 | Check whether the overdue count is falling |
| 7 | 0–5 | Resume a few only if the queue is stable |

If the queue is not falling, keep new cards paused or enlarge the time block only if it does not harm other priorities.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I do the newest or oldest reviews first?

Prioritize relevance and urgency over age. Within an equally important deck, following the app's due order is usually reasonable.

### Should I delete overdue cards?

Delete cards that are duplicates, obsolete or not worth future time. Keep important cards even if they are overdue; relearn them through honest retrieval.

### How long does it take to clear a backlog?

Divide overdue cards by your careful daily review capacity, then allow extra time for failed cards to return. The recovery can take days or weeks, and that is normal.

### Can I keep adding cards for my current class?

Only if they are urgent and the amount is small enough that the total queue still declines. Save non-urgent candidates outside the active schedule.

### What if opening the app feels overwhelming?

Hide the total count if possible, select one high-priority deck and use a 10-minute timer. Success is completing the block, not clearing the system.

## The practical takeaway

A review backlog is solved by subtraction, prioritization and repeated small sessions.

Pause additions. Keep the valuable cards. Repair the bad ones. Review within a boundary you can repeat tomorrow. [biu's spaced-repetition system](/spaced-repetition) can decide when cards return, but your time budget should decide how large the system is allowed to become.
